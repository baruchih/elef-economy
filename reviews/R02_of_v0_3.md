## 1. The reimbursement shortfall branch still has incompatible payout instructions

The no-shortfall formula is clear. The issue is what happens when emission must first repay advances.

Section 4.3 says to use the case pool to repay deferred reimbursements and operating-account advances **before paying fees**. Its next step nevertheless says that each surviving reviewer receives its own emission share, plus a share of the remaining deposit. Those full emission shares may already have been partly consumed by repayment. agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_3

### A concrete case

Assume:

| Item | Credits |
|---|---:|
| Verification deposit | 10 |
| Three reviews costing five each | 15 |
| Advance from \(\Omega\) | 5 |
| Emission allocated to surviving reviews | 6 |

The six-credit emission allocation is illustrative; it is not a new proposed default.

At vesting, the case pool is:

\[
(10-15)^+ + 6 = 6.
\]

Repaying the five-credit advance leaves:

\[
6-5=\boxed{1}
\]

for reviewer fees.

**The reviewers cannot then receive their full six credits of emission shares as well.**

The correction does not require a new mechanism. It requires the final allocation to operate on the **remaining pool after repayment**, with a stated rule for distributing that remainder. The current no-shortfall equation can remain exactly that: a no-shortfall equation.

### “Every case repays its advance” is still too strong

Section 4.3 calls \(\Omega\) a float and says every advance is repaid by its case. Section 10.4 allows an insufficiently funded reviewer claim to be paid from **future service-fee receipts**, first in, first out. agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_3 agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_3

These describe different assurances.

A case with a zero verification deposit and insufficient emission does not generate enough funds to reimburse every review. Future service fees can cover the obligation, but that means the operating account bears funding risk across cases. It is not guaranteed case-by-case repayment.

**That can be a valid chosen rule. It just needs to be described as the rule actually chosen.**

The debt is also capped at \(K\) **per review**, not globally. Many unpaid review claims can accumulate.

### Deferred payment conflicts with the finality invariant

The invariant says:

> “No funds move after vesting on account of that case.”

But a deferred reimbursement paid from a later service-fee receipt is precisely such a movement. agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_3 agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_3

The intended distinction appears to be:

## 3. Indexing can make a later court fee exceed the bond already locked

### The timing problem

Suppose a reviewer locks five credits when \(v=1\).

Later, before a challenge, the credit value is \(0.25\). If the court fee is calculated at that later value:

\[
D_{\text{later}}=\frac{2}{0.25}=8.
\]

The previously locked bond is still five credits.

**The nominal defaults \(5\ge2\) do not guarantee that the actual locked bond covers the subsequently charged fee.**

The missing rule is the **valuation time for an existing commitment**. Are its relevant amounts fixed when the review begins, when the case is awarded, or recalculated later under some explicit restriction?

## 4. Reviewer timeouts invalidate the lifetime limit of three distinct reviewers

Reassigning a timed-out review is a sensible rule. But it conflicts with the invariant that a case never consumes more than three distinct reviewers. agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_3 agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_3 agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_3

This sequence is permitted:

| Assignment | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Reviewer 1 | Times out; review withdrawn |
| Reviewer 2 | Replaces it and approves |
| Reviewer 3 | Audits and disagrees |
| Reviewer 4 | Supplies the additional appeal assessment |

That is **four distinct reviewers for one case**, despite only one challenge round.

Repeated timeouts can make the lifetime count higher still.

**Review positions needed to process a case.**  
**Distinct actors that may have occupied those positions over the case’s lifetime.**

The reserve bound and the recorded invariant need to say which they concern. The audit/dispute reuse rule handles duplicated review positions in the successful path; it does not establish a lifetime bound in the presence of replacement.

## 5. The corrected reserve argument still contains an incorrect price conclusion

The correction that **an idle economy does not necessarily drain its reserve** is good.

But this remaining implication is not established:

\[
\mathrm{Res}_t\to0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p_r\to\infty.
\]

The same section explicitly defines resource purchases as neutral to credit value: the reserve falls, and the corresponding credits are burned. agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_3

Under your equations, for a purchase costing \(c\):

\[
v'=\frac{\mathrm{Res}-c}{M-c/v}.
\]

Since \(v=\mathrm{Res}/M\), this gives:

\[
\boxed{v'=v}
\]

while the balances remain positive.

For example, with reserve 100 and supply 100, spending 20 real units leaves reserve 80 and supply 80. The credit value remains one, and the resource’s credit price remains unchanged.

**Emission can dilute the credit. Resource redemption by itself does not.**

The source can therefore support the narrower claim:

> Without replenishment, resource purchases consume finite real backing, and further purchases eventually become impossible if sufficient backing is unavailable.

It does not establish that this must happen through infinitely rising credit prices. The exact terminal treatment of zero reserve or zero supply also needs to avoid an undefined \(0/0\).

This is a correction to the stated consequence, not an objection to using a backed credit or having the requester hold the “plug.”

### One qualification to the revised deterrence claim

The numerical thresholds in §7.4 are correct under its assumptions: \(G^V>18\) at \(p=0.5\), and \(G^V>48\) at \(p=0.2\), when \(b=0.2\) and \(\bar f=2\). agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_3
