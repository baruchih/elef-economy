The 44-credit escrow and five-credit emission allocation are already fully assigned. **The six-credit reimbursement needs another source.** The example explicitly omits the orchestrator service fee, so it cannot explain that source within this example.

## 2. The new reviewer bond does not secure the reviewer’s entire liability

An overturned original reviewer can lose its **five-credit bond** and owe the winning challenger **two credits of court-fee reimbursement**. But only the bond is explicitly locked. The document also prohibits overdrafts while deferring insolvency. agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_2 agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_2 agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_2

Consider an ordinary, non-collusive sequence:

> A reviewer completes its review. Its bond remains locked. It uses its remaining unlocked balance on subsequent work. The original verdict is then overturned.

It has five locked credits, but the required financial consequences total seven. Burning the bond leaves nothing with which to reimburse the challenger.

That means **collectibility is already on the critical path of one appeal**, not only a future bankruptcy question.

## 3. The claim that a reviewer who approves without reading is automatically unprofitable is incorrect

Section 7.2 appropriately says that deterrence must be checked. Section 7.4 then states:

> “A stamper gets its near-zero costs back and nothing else; with audits it is negative in expectation.”

That conclusion does not follow. **An unexamined approval that survives still receives its fee.** There is no rule that automatically identifies whether the reviewer actually did adequate work. agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_2

Here is a counterexample derived from the proposed rules—not a simulation result.

Assume:

- A reviewer approves everything without meaningful inspection.
- The audit probability is \(p=0.5\).
- Twenty percent of submissions actually fail the assessment.
- An audit catches every failed submission it examines.
- There are no requester disputes in this example.
- The reviewer’s average fee conditional on survival is two credits.
- The bond is five credits.

The chance of being caught is:

\[
p b=0.5\times0.2=0.1.
\]

Ignoring negligible execution costs and separate reputation effects, its expected monetary result is:

\[
\mathbb E[\Delta W]
=(1-pb)\bar f-pbG^V
\]

\[
=0.9\times2-0.1\times5
=\boxed{+1.3}.
\]

The two-credit fee here is an illustrative possible average of the derived payout—not a proposal to restore the old fixed fee.

This **does not prove that careless approval is the best strategy**. It demonstrates that the stated rules do not automatically make it negative in expectation.

The sound conclusion is narrower:

> **Audits, forfeited fees, bonds, and reputation consequences may make inadequate review unattractive. Whether they do depends on the actual payout, detection, and opportunity-cost conditions.**

That is already close to what §7.2 asks the simulation to investigate. I would keep that as a hypothesis and remove the stronger assertion from §7.4.

Also, “the verdict survived” and “the reviewer was right” should remain different descriptions. Otherwise, the specification slips back into treating procedural survival as correctness.

## 5. Delegation is much better defined—but its guarantees do not yet compose cleanly

The nested-request structure is a strong addition. The difficult part is that the parent actor now has **two different responsibilities**:

> A owes a result to its requester, and A judges the result supplied by B.

The specification says A’s judgments of nested results are backed by A’s upstream guarantee instead of a separate reviewer bond. It also says funds cannot be double-pledged. agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_2 agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_2

That creates an unresolved question:

**What exactly can happen to that one guarantee when several responsibilities fail?**

For example, A incorrectly accepts B’s result, and A also fails its own root task. Does A’s guarantee secure its review judgment, its delivery obligation, or a shared maximum exposure across both?

## What is still on the critical path

Some items listed as off the critical path are not off it under the current rules.

**Physical tick duration** can remain arbitrary in an abstract simulation. **Ordering at a deadline** cannot: it determines whether a challenge is admitted, whether funds vest, and which assignment occurs first.

Likewise, full bankruptcy economics can remain deferred. **What happens when a reviewer owes seven credits but only has five** needs an answer for the existing settlement procedure.

A never-submitted task or never-completed review also still needs an explicit treatment if the loop is intended to terminate. The new response, revision, evidence, and subcontract-wait timers do not cover every such case. The document retains progress caveats but nevertheless concludes that every parameter needed for a task is supplied. agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_2 agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_2 agentic_economics_loop_spec_v0_2

Finally, a first run needs an initialization: positive reserve and money-supply values for \(v_0\), an origin for initial actor balances, and resource-cost inputs. The worked examples assume starting balances; they do not define that initialization.

## Overall assessment

**I would keep the new direction.** In particular, I would preserve requester-funded commitments, contested approvals, delayed vesting, separate reliability and duty credit, and explicit nested contracts.

My concern is not that you made decisions too early. You deliberately chose provisional defaults, which is appropriate. The issue is that **a default value does not resolve conflicting transitions or identify a missing source of funds**.

I would describe v0.2 as:

> **A substantially more complete protocol proposal, with its major directions selected, but with funding, collateral, finality, and nested-settlement inconsistencies still to reconcile.**

The distinction matters. Some questions now genuinely can be studied through simulation—whether fees encourage good reviewing, whether price pressure works, whether demand supports verification. Other issues, such as who pays a reimbursement or what happens to an already released guarantee, require a rule before a simulator can evaluate anything faithfully.

**The model has moved forward. The strongest next revision would not add more mechanisms; it would make the selected mechanisms agree with one another.**
