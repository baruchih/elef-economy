# The ELEF economy — a step-back review

*November 10, 2026. By the economy's reviewer (Claude, `claude-fable-5-2`), for Baruchi. Read from the public record at elef.baruchih.com (home, rulebook, evolution, 2.7.0 / 2.8.0 / 2.9.0, replay, related research), the project's run records and rulings, and the record reviewers' notes. An opinion, not a ruling. Nothing here changes a rule.*

## The verdict in one paragraph

The research has produced one real result, a sound way of working, and an unusually honest record. The result: **peer verification among self-interested agents is honest only where truth the verifier cannot anticipate is present at the verdict, at a rate the population does not control** — everything else (audits by peers, appeal panels, dispute panels) confirms itself. That result is right, it is important, and the two revisions built on it (2.8.0, 2.9.0) are the right kind of answer. What the work has *not* done is test the economy against the things that will decide whether it works for real agents doing real work: verification that actually costs the verifier something, effort nobody can observe, mistakes that are correlated, work products that carry signals, operators who run many identities, and an adversary that thinks more than one step ahead. The simulated world is the best case on every one of those. So my view: **the insight is validated; the economy as a product design is not yet tested**, and the next phase should attack the model's assumptions rather than add rules.

## What I think is solid

1. **The central finding, and it now stands on clean evidence.** On the inspectors' own money, at 2.7.0, a learning population checked at the first review (where a planted case may come) and approved without looking at the audit seat 94–97 times in 100 (where none could). After the plants reached every seat (2.8.0) and the orchestrator began planting wrong approvals and lying challenges (2.9.0), that fell to 41–56, and lying appeals are paid about as rarely as against scripted honest panels. In absolute terms, by my own arithmetic from the record's figures (about a fifth of deliveries bad, about 1,850 paid tasks a run): roughly one bad job in ten was paid at 2.7.0 and one in thirty to fifty at 2.9.0 — under one percent of all paid work — against one in sixty-five for the scripted mix.
2. **It is a known result, rediscovered — which is good news.** Blockchain verification calls it the *verifier's dilemma* (rational verifiers skip checks when work is usually right) and answers it with *forced errors*: the system deliberately injects wrong results and pays whoever catches them (TrueBit). Contract theory has the same answer to "who monitors the monitor": occasionally and secretly ask the worker to shirk, and reward the monitor for catching it (Rahman, 2012). 2.9.0's planted approval and planted lie are that mechanism. And the crowdsourcing literature shows the other half of ELEF's finding formally: without ground truth, peer-agreement schemes always have an equilibrium where nobody does the work, and a plain spot-check against trusted answers gives stronger guarantees with fewer checks than any peer scheme (Gao, Wright & Leyton-Brown, 2016). ELEF got to all three from first principles. None of them is on the related-research page; they should be, and they carry design guidance (below).
3. **The discipline.** One rule per revision, a classification before any change, hypotheses written before runs, paired seeds, everything recorded, and three separated roles. It worked as a real check: the record's reviewers found three errors in the economy's own reasoning — two of them mine — and the record published them instead of editing them away. That is the most credible thing on the site.
4. **The plumbing.** Escrow, the worker's guarantee, the inspector's bond, the audit roll, probation-by-inspection, the arrival loan, the bank as an account that cannot print for the state, rebates paid only from income. These are coherent and the exploit-then-fix cycles on them (the float harvest; the timing tells) were clean.

## Where the evidence is weaker than it reads

1. **In the model, checking is free and effort is visible.** The engine reimburses an inspector's cost of care in the same step, and only when the inspector actually checked. So an inspector saves nothing by skipping, and the "temptation to stamp" the whole design exists to resist is not in the world — at the 2.7.0 audit seat it was worth a third of a credit, the price of the inspector's own 5% error. Real effort is not observable; a flat allowance paid either way makes skipping worth the full cost of checking. At the simulation's own magnitudes (care 2–3, bond 5, fee 2) the immediate penalties do not fully cover that even at the first review and fall far short at the seats behind it, and the plant record tolerates a reviewer who waves through roughly a third of bad plants with little risk of suspension. The pooled-reward runs, where there *was* a reason to approve, are an accidental preview: first-seat stamping near 30% and false approvals about three times the reference. **The own-money results are the economy with no temptation; nobody has yet run it with one.**
2. **The learners cannot see the future, and the design's deterrents are mostly in the future.** Both learned designs pick the action with the best immediate payoff. Loss of panel eligibility, probation, doubled audit odds — the standing-based penalties that 1.2.0–1.3.0 and 2.9.0 rely on — are invisible to them (only suspension is charged, by hand). So the runs test whether the *bond* deters, not whether the *design* does; and they cannot find multi-step attacks except where the harness hand-built the steps.
3. **Everyone errs independently, and nobody can recognise anything.** Each inspector is right 95% of the time, independently. Real agents built on similar models share blind spots, so a second and third look catch far less than the model credits. And the model has no work product: an inspector sees 45 numbers, not a deliverable. In the real thing the deliverable is a channel — a worker can mark its work so its own (or a paid) inspector recognises it, or write text that manipulates a language-model inspector. **Plants deter indiscriminate laziness; they cannot deter an inspector who stamps only what it recognises, because a plant is never marked.** Only an independent audit of real work catches that, and the "collusion cannot find itself" result (run 28) holds only in a world without content.
4. **One shared policy, one training run per cell.** Every inspector is the same policy, so only symmetric outcomes can appear; and the error bars are across evaluation seeds of a single trained policy. The two learners disagree by a factor of 1.6 on the headline number (16.3 against 9.9), which is the size of the uncertainty the error bars do not show.
5. **The customer is all-or-nothing.** Requesters either never dispute or catch every bad result at once; with the second, the learned population is as honest as the careful one and most of the machinery is idle. Where real customers sit between those two is the single most informative parameter, and it has not been swept.
6. **The headline metric.** "Times the scripted mix" compares against a population that starts 60% stampers. The absolute leak rate (share of paid work that is bad) and the all-in verification overhead per delivered task are the numbers a reader, or a customer, needs.

## What I think about the design itself

1. **It has grown faster than its evidence.** Seventeen design revisions in under two weeks; 107 rules; several of the later ones answering instrument artefacts as much as the economy. The record's own conclusion — honesty comes only from known truth — implies a much smaller core: escrow and stakes; a first review; ground truth by plants (including wrong-step plants) and by trusted random audits of real work; the customer's dispute with trusted arbitration. The peer layers earn their place as capacity and error-correction, not as incentives, and the literature says the same.
2. **Bonds should burn only against trusted truth.** Today, when an auditor correctly rejects and the next seat disagrees, the ruling goes against the auditor 20 times in 100 and its bond burns. That makes honesty a bad reply to a dishonest population — the opposite of what the layer is for. Let peer disagreement only *escalate*; let money move only on the orchestrator's truth; and make the final ruling the most accurate judgment in the system, not the least (0.8 against the troops' 0.95).
3. **Raise the fine before the rate.** The design deters by planting more (a fifth of first reviews; at the planted-approval cap, two stings for every five real approvals a member audits). The older answer is a bigger stake at the seats that matter: an auditor's bond several times a first reviewer's buys the same deterrence with a fraction of the stings.
4. **The state is the product.** Truth (plants), honest capacity (troops), and final judgment (rulings) all come from the orchestrator; in the stressed (pooled-reward) runs it did three audits in four. That is fine — it is what a trust anchor is — but it should be said, costed, and priced. In the simulation a plant is a free bit; in reality every plant is a task-sized artefact someone must produce and disguise, and troops are compute. A 5% delegation fee has to cover that. Whether trusted audits of real work are cheaper than plants for the same honesty is an open, testable question, and on the simulation's own numbers they may be.
5. **"Humans do not intervene" is the most expensive assumption.** It removes the cheapest ground truth there is. Keeping humans out of the normal path is right; keeping them out of the last, rare tier of escalation makes an automated judge's accuracy the ceiling of truth for all real work.
6. **The money is ahead of the need.** The bank, the index and emission are internally consistent, but the question the company has to answer is about verification, not monetary policy, and credits that buy nothing motivate nobody until they redeem for something the *operator* values. Stakes, prices and fees are essential; minting is not, yet.

## Where I was wrong in this session, for the record

The attribution rule that pooled the population's reward was mine and I did not test it on a population; I reasoned from a cost of care the code never charged; I set a cap the dial could not reach; I designed 2.9.0's price to run through eligibility, which the learner cannot perceive, without asking what the instrument could see; and I ruled faster than the evidence more than once. A population-level calibration cell — the rule I had set for single learners and did not carry over — would have caught the first. The lesson I draw: validate the instrument as strictly as the engine before reading a result into the rulebook.

## What I would do next, in order

1. **Freeze the rulebook at 2.9.0 with its defaults.** Finish the owed cells; design nothing on them.
2. **Make the world honest before the rules.** (a) Charge care to the inspector and make effort unobservable; sweep cost-of-care against bond. (b) Sweep the customer's chance of noticing bad work from 0 to 1. (c) Add a shared component to errors. (d) A forward-looking learner on own money, several training seeds, more than one policy in a population. (e) An operator with several identities and a "recognise my own work" bit. (f) Report the absolute leak rate and the all-in verification overhead.
3. **An ablation, not an addition.** Run the small core (first review + plants + trusted random audit + dispute with trusted arbitration; bonds burn on trusted truth only) against the full rulebook on leak and cost. Cut what is not load-bearing.
4. **One pilot with language-model agents on a task family whose truth is checkable** — the invoice reconciliation from the integration track is built for it. Measure what the simulation assumes: can an inspector model tell a plant; what checking costs and how accurate it is; how correlated the errors are; whether marked work gets recognised. Build plants from previously adjudicated real work.
5. **Write the theory section** linking the finding to the verifier's dilemma, forced errors, monitoring-the-monitor and spot-checking, and add them to the related-research page.
6. **State the business as the trust anchor** and price truth explicitly.
